4.1 What is Information Security Risk?

The probability that a threat will exploit a vulnerability and inflict tangible harm.

🎲
Plain English Definition

Risk is the possibility of something bad happening and the damage it could cause. It connects technical vulnerabilities directly to operational and financial consequences.

Institutional Risk Manifestations

  • 💳 Hackers exfiltrate 50,000 credit cards and social security records.
  • ⚡ Unscheduled storage array failure paralyzes university operations.
  • 🔒 Ransomware locks medical hospital histories during active surgeries.
  • 🗑️ An employee accidentally truncates the main production database table.
  • 🌐 Web portal crashes during time-sensitive scholarship registration.

Why Conduct Risk Assessment?

  • 🎯 Pinpoints which crown-jewel systems need immediate defense.
  • 💰 Prevents overspending on low-risk assets and underspending on critical assets.
  • 📉 Drastically lowers the probability and severity of incidents.
  • 🤝 Satisfies corporate compliance, legal mandates, and audits.
  • 🏢 Enables senior leadership to make justified, data-driven security investments.

4.3 The 8-Step Risk Assessment Lifecycle

A rigorous, repeatable methodology applied across modern cybersecurity frameworks.

Step 1

Identify Assets

Catalog databases, web portals, servers, networks, and confidential records.

Step 2

Identify Threats

Identify threat actors, malware, DoS attacks, power surges, and human error.

Step 3

Identify Flaws

Audit weak passwords, unpatched libraries, missing MFA, or open database ports.

Step 4

Analyze Likelihood

Estimate probability: Low (unlikely), Medium (possible), High (frequent).

Step 5

Analyze Impact

Quantify business fallout: Low (minor inconvenience) to High (catastrophic).

Step 6

Evaluate & Rank

Plot onto risk matrix to separate critical emergency hazards from tolerable bugs.

Step 7

Apply Treatments

Select from: Avoidance, Mitigation, Transfer, or Acceptance.

Step 8

Monitor & Review

Maintain continuous audit logging and quarterly re-assessment reviews.

4.6 Four Risk Treatment Strategies

The four formal decisions an organization can take when addressing evaluated risks.

Strategy 1

🚫 Risk Avoidance

Completely eliminating the system, service, or business activity that introduces the hazard.

Real-World Example: A university decomissions an unpatchable legacy Windows XP payroll machine entirely and ceases its use.

Strategy 2

🛡️ Risk Mitigation (Reduction)

Implementing security controls and technical safeguards to lower likelihood or suppress impact.

Real-World Example: Deploying MFA, Web Application Firewalls (WAF), and automated daily cloud backups to protect enrollment databases.

Strategy 3

🤝 Risk Transfer (Sharing)

Shifting portions of the financial or operational burden of a breach to an external entity.

Real-World Example: Procuring a $5,000,000 Cybersecurity Insurance policy. (Caution: Insurance covers monetary cost; it cannot fix data leaks or restore lost trust!)

Strategy 4

📝 Risk Acceptance (Retention)

Acknowledging and tolerating a residual risk when the cost of countermeasures outweighs potential damage.

Real-World Example: Accepting the risk of a 5-minute campus cafeteria Wi-Fi restart because fixing it requires a $100,000 architectural overhaul.

4.7 Major Enterprise Risk Assessment Frameworks

The voluntary standards that structure cybersecurity risk governance globally.

Framework 1

NIST Risk Management Framework (RMF)

National Institute of Standards and Technology • Special Publication 800-37

Official NIST RMF ↗

The NIST RMF provides a 7-step continuous lifecycle for securing federal and commercial information systems:

1. Prepare: Organizational risk context.
2. Categorize: Classify system impact (FIPS 199).
3. Select: Choose security controls (SP 800-53).
4. Implement: Apply technical and administrative controls.
5. Assess: Verify effectiveness via audit.
6. Authorize: Formally approve system for operation.
7. Monitor: Continuously monitor control changes and new vulnerabilities.
Framework 2

NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF 2.0)

The global benchmark for managing and communicating cybersecurity risk.

Official NIST CSF 2.0 ↗

Updated to version 2.0 with the addition of the overarching Govern function across 6 core pillars:

1. GOVERN (GV)

Establish security governance, policies, roles, and enterprise oversight.

2. IDENTIFY (ID)

Catalog physical and software assets, dependencies, and risk exposure.

3. PROTECT (PR)

Deploy safeguards: access controls, encryption, awareness training.

4. DETECT (DE)

Monitor networks to uncover anomalies, intrusions, and malware events.

5. RESPOND (RS)

Execute incident containment, eradication, and stakeholder notifications.

6. RECOVER (RC)

Restore damaged services, rebuild databases, and extract lessons learned.

Framework 3

ISO/IEC 27001 (ISMS)

An internationally auditable standard defining requirements for an Information Security Management System (ISMS). Focuses on leadership commitment, risk treatment, continuous improvement (PDCA), and Annex A security controls.

Application: Universities or companies seek ISO 27001 certification to prove to clients and governments that their security operations are rigorously managed.
Framework 4

OCTAVE Methodology

Operationally Critical Threat, Asset, and Vulnerability Evaluation developed by Carnegie Mellon University SEI. It is an internal self-directed risk assessment methodology tailored for organizational business assets.

Application: Prioritizes institutional crown jewels (e.g. medical patient records, university financial accounts) and internal operational risk ownership.

4.8 Comparative Synthesis of Voluntary Frameworks

Distinguishing when and why an organization deploys each standard.

Framework Primary Purpose Core Structural Focus Primary Audience
NIST RMF Manage risk throughout system lifecycles 7-step sequential risk lifecycle process US Federal agencies, defense, enterprise contractors
NIST CSF 2.0 Organize and improve risk outcomes 6 Core Functions (Govern, ID, PR, DE, RS, RC) Global commercial, academic, & critical infrastructure
ISO/IEC 27001 Formal auditable ISMS certification Organizational management policies & Annex A International corporations, B2B cloud SaaS vendors
